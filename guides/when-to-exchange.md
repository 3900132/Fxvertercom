---

The honest answer first: **nobody reliably predicts exchange rates** — not banks, not analysts, not algorithms. Professional currency traders with better data than any of us are right barely more often than a coin flip over short horizons. So the goal is not to guess the perfect moment; it is to avoid the worst ones and let simple mechanics do the rest.

## What actually moves rates in the short term

Daily moves are driven by news: central bank decisions, inflation prints, employment figures and political events. These are scheduled or sudden, and the market reacts within seconds. By the time a "strong dollar" headline reaches you, the move has usually already happened.

Over weeks and months, rates drift with interest-rate differentials and economic momentum. Over years, currencies tend to revert toward what their economies justify — but "years" is not a planning horizon for a trip or an invoice.

## Use alerts instead of predictions

Most banking and transfer apps offer **rate alerts**: you set the level you would consider good, and you get notified when the market touches it. This replaces guessing with a rule. If the rate never reaches your level, you still converted — just without the luck. If it does, you did slightly better than the market's average user, which compounds over a year of transfers.

## Split large conversions

If you are moving a large amount — tuition, a property deposit, a business payment — consider **splitting it into parts** over days or weeks. You will not catch the top or the bottom, but you will get the **average** of the period, which is usually close to the fair rate and removes the risk of converting everything on the single worst day of the month.

## The worst moments to convert

- **Airport counters** — the worst margins in the industry, at any hour
- **Last-minute deadline pressure** — if you must convert today, you have zero negotiating power
- **Holiday rushes** — demand spikes around major holidays in the destination country
- **Right after a dramatic headline** — volatility is highest exactly when everyone is reacting; spreads widen

## For businesses: stop timing, start hedging

Companies with recurring foreign invoices should not play the timing game at all. The standard tools are simple: invoice in your own currency where possible, keep a multi-currency balance to pay and receive naturally, and for large predictable payments ask your bank about a **forward contract** — it locks today's rate for a future date, converting an unpredictable cost into a known one.

## The takeaway

Set an alert, avoid the known-bad moments, split big amounts, and get on with your life. The time you don't spend watching charts is worth more than the fraction of a percent you might catch by watching them.
