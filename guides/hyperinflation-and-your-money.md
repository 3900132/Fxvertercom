Hyperinflation is not "very high inflation". Economists usually draw the line at inflation above **50% per month** — prices doubling roughly every 83 days. At that pace, money stops functioning as money: nobody wants to hold it, salaries are spent within hours of being paid, and prices are quoted in a more stable foreign currency instead. Argentina, Turkey, Venezuela, Zimbabwe and Weimar Germany all offer versions of this story.

## Where hyperinflation comes from

Hyperinflation is almost always a **fiscal phenomenon**. A government spends far more than it collects in taxes, cannot borrow credibly, and fills the gap by printing money. Each round of money-printing weakens the currency, prices rise, the government needs even more money, and the loop tightens. Wars, collapsed tax systems and unpayable debts are the usual triggers. It is a loss of trust first and a monetary event second: once people believe the currency will keep shrinking, they get rid of it as fast as they can, which makes it shrink faster.

## What it does to the exchange rate

The exchange rate is where the loss of trust becomes visible daily:

- The official rate **lags and lies**. Governments facing capital flight often impose official exchange rates and capital controls; the market rate on the street can then be several times worse. The official chart shows a staircase of discrete devaluations while the real rate slides continuously.
- **Foreign currency substitutes for local money** in savings and big-ticket pricing — dollars, euros, or increasingly stable regional currencies. Property, cars and long-term contracts get quoted in the hard currency.
- **The chart loses meaning.** A log-scale chart of a hyperinflating currency is a cliff. Percentage moves that look huge in normal markets are noise; the useful number is how much purchasing power the currency lost this month.

## The practical rules people actually use

People living through it converge on similar behaviour, which is the best available advice:

- **Convert earnings quickly.** Wages are changed into a stable currency or spent on essentials the same day. Holding local cash for weeks is an unforced loss.
- **Price in the stable unit.** Freelancers and businesses that can bill in dollars or euros effectively insure their income; those who must bill locally raise prices frequently just to stand still.
- **Keep savings in assets, not cash.** Foreign-currency accounts, real estate, durable goods — anything that holds value better than the collapsing unit. This is not investment advice so much as damage control.
- **Expect informal dollarisation.** Even where foreign currency is not legal tender, it becomes the de facto unit of account for anything expensive.
- **Beware the day after.** When stabilisation comes — a new currency, a peg, a credible central bank — old notes become souvenirs. Conversion schemes and deadlines matter; people who miss them lose everything on paper.

## If you are sending money into or out of a crisis

Remittances often become a lifeline during currency collapses, and they carry their own traps:

- The gap between **official and market rates** determines how much your transfer is really worth. A transfer at the official rate can be worth half of what the recipient could get by other routes.
- Recipients often prefer **receiving in a stable currency** (a foreign-currency account or cash pickup) rather than local notes that melt.
- Fees quoted as "zero" can hide the spread between official and real rates — compare by asking how much the recipient can actually buy with what arrives.
- Transfer corridors into sanctioned or unstable economies can be slow, expensive or interrupted; test with a small amount before relying on a route.

## The bigger lesson for everyone else

Hyperinflation is the extreme end of a spectrum everyone stands on. The same mechanics — who bears inflation risk, how quickly money loses value, what a "safe" currency is — run quietly in ordinary economies at 2% and loudly at 2,000%. The practical takeaways generalise:

- **Cash is a depreciating asset** in every inflationary environment; the question is only the speed.
- **The currency you earn in and the currency you spend in should match**, or you are running an open exchange-rate position with your salary.
- **Reference rates are your lie detector.** Comparing the official rate against the mid-market rate — which is exactly what a converter like Fxverter shows — reveals margins, controls and distortions faster than any news report.

Money is a technology for storing trust. Hyperinflation is what happens when that trust is withdrawn all at once — and its lessons about diversification and honest rates are worth learning long before you need them.
