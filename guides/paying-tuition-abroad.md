A four-year degree abroad is one of the largest currency conversions most families ever make. Tuition of 30,000 units per year, converted twice a year for four years, means eight large transfers — and a 3% hidden margin on each one silently adds up to several thousand units over the degree. The good news: with a little planning, most of that cost is avoidable.

## Know the real cost of a transfer

As with any international payment, the sticker fee is the small part. What matters is the **total cost**: the upfront fee plus the gap between the rate you are offered and the mid-market rate. Before paying a tuition bill, do this once:

1. Check the mid-market rate for your currency pair (Fxverter shows it live).
2. Ask your bank or transfer provider exactly **how much will arrive in the university's currency** if you send the invoiced amount.
3. If what arrives is short of the invoice, the difference is the university's "shortfall" — more on that below — or someone's hidden margin.

A difference of 2–4% is common with banks; specialised services are usually much closer to the mid-market rate.

## Watch out for the "recipient pays" trap

International wires can pass through **correspondent banks**, each deducting its own fee. Universities know this, which is why many ask you to pay a so-called **shared charges** arrangement (OUR, in SWIFT language) or use a dedicated tuition-payment platform. If you choose "beneficiary pays" and intermediaries take their cut, the university receives less than the invoice and will chase your student for the shortfall — a stressful email you want to avoid.

Some universities partner with tuition-payment platforms that lock the rate for a few days and guarantee the university receives the full amount. Compare those quotes against a specialist transfer service too; convenience platforms are not always the cheapest.

## Timing: when to convert

- **Tuition is invoiced in the destination currency**, so your real question is when to buy that currency. Exchange a few days before the deadline rather than the same morning; rates move, and deadline-day panic conversions get the worst quotes.
- **Do not try to time the market.** Waiting months for a "better rate" is speculation with money you owe. A sensible compromise for large sums: split the conversion into two or three tranches over a couple of weeks and average the result.
- **Rate alerts** on any banking app will tell you when your pair touches a level you consider acceptable — automation beats refreshing charts at midnight.

## Living costs: set up the right accounts

For day-to-day spending, the cheapest conversion is usually the one you do least often, at the best rate, in the local currency:

- A **multi-currency account** or a card that converts at close to the mid-market rate removes most day-to-day FX cost. Get it sorted before departure, not in a queue at the airport.
- **Always choose to pay in the local currency** at card terminals and ATMs. The "pay in your home currency" option is dynamic currency conversion, typically 3–10% worse than letting your own bank convert.
- Withdraw **larger amounts less often** if your card charges a fixed ATM fee; withdraw from bank-owned ATMs, not standalone machines in shops.
- Keep a **small emergency buffer** in the local currency for the weeks when something breaks — but avoid stockpiling large cash amounts.

## Funding from two currencies? Match money to spending

Many students receive support in the home currency but spend abroad. Every unnecessary double conversion — home currency to a third currency to the local one — adds a margin at each step. Where possible:

- Transfer **directly** from the home currency to the destination currency in one hop.
- If relatives send money regularly, agree on a **fixed monthly schedule** rather than ad-hoc urgent transfers; urgency costs money.
- Keep records: for tax and scholarship purposes you may need to show how much was transferred and at what rate. Screenshot the quote on the day.

## A checklist for the first semester

- Compare at least two providers on the received amount for your first tuition payment — the habit pays for the whole degree.
- Pay tuition with charges arranged so the university receives the **full invoiced amount**.
- Open a low-FX-cost card or multi-currency account before departure.
- Set a rate alert for your pair; split large conversions into tranches.
- Always pay in the local currency abroad; refuse terminal currency conversion.
- Keep a one-page record of each transfer: date, amount, rate, reference number.

Exchange rates will move during your degree — that is out of your control. What is fully in your control is paying 0–1% instead of 3–5% on every transfer, and never discovering a "shortfall" email from the bursar's office in week one.
