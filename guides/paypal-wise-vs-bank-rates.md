When you send money internationally, three costs decide how much arrives: the **upfront fee**, the **exchange-rate margin**, and any **intermediate fees** along the way. Most people compare the first and never see the second — yet the rate margin is usually the largest of the three. This guide shows how the big providers structure those costs and how to compare them in two minutes.

## First, a rule: always compare the received amount

Forget advertised fees and headline rates. The only honest comparison is this: **send the same amount to the same country on the same day and look at exactly how much lands in the recipient's account, in their currency, after everything is deducted.** Ask two or three providers for that one number and pick the biggest. Because rates move daily, repeat the exercise before your next transfer — last year's cheapest option is not automatically today's.

## How PayPal converts currency

PayPal is built for convenience, not for cheap conversion. When a payment crosses currencies, PayPal applies a **spread on top of its wholesale reference rate** — a retail markup that typically runs in the 3–4% range for many corridors, and more for some smaller currencies. On top of that sit possible fixed fees for certain transfers and, for cross-border commercial payments, additional percentage charges.

The practical consequences:

- The rate PayPal shows you at checkout is a **take-it-or-leave-it quote** — there is no visible breakdown of how much margin is inside it.
- For small purchases the convenience is often worth it. For a **large transfer — tuition, rent, a car — a 3% markup is real money**: on 10,000 units it can cost more than 300.
- If you keep a balance in the recipient's currency, or the recipient can invoice you in theirs, you can sometimes sidestep conversion entirely.

## How Wise converts currency

Wise (formerly TransferWise) popularised a different model: charge a **visible fee** and convert at the **mid-market rate** — the same rate you see on Fxverter or any financial news site. The fee is shown before you confirm, usually a percentage that declines with size plus a small fixed component.

What that means in practice:

- The received-amount quote is generally **close to what a pure mid-market conversion would give**, with the fee being the entire cost.
- For most major corridors the total cost tends to be lower than bank wires and PayPal, especially on mid-sized transfers.
- Multi-currency balances let you hold, receive and convert later — useful if you can wait for a better rate, though waiting is a decision, not a strategy (see our guide on exchange-rate risk).

## How a traditional bank wire converts currency

Banks rarely charge a dramatic visible fee for a SWIFT transfer — the cost hides elsewhere. Three layers are common:

- A **wire fee** for sending, sometimes another for receiving.
- An **exchange-rate margin** that is often 1–4% away from the mid-market rate, rarely shown as a percentage anywhere.
- **Correspondent-bank deductions**: international wires can pass through one or two intermediate banks, each taking a cut before the money arrives. The recipient receives less than you sent, with no line item explaining where it went.

The correspondent-bank layer is the one people discover by accident. It is also the hardest to predict, because routing depends on the currency pair and the banks involved.

## The comparison in one table

| Provider type | Visible fee | Rate margin | Hidden intermediaries | Typical total cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional bank wire | Moderate | Often 1–4% | Correspondent banks may deduct | Usually highest |
| PayPal | Low to none advertised | Roughly 3–4%+ | Baked into the quote | High on large amounts |
| Specialised services (Wise and similar) | Clear, shown upfront | ≈ 0% (mid-market) | None | Usually lowest |

Exact numbers vary by corridor, amount and promotions, so treat the table as a map of *where the money hides*, not a price list.

## When the "expensive" option is actually fine

- **Small, infrequent purchases**: a 3% margin on a 30-unit payment is less than the coffee you buy while thinking about it. Convenience wins.
- **Urgent transfers**: when speed matters more than cost, whatever clears today can be worth the premium.
- **Stable salary arrangements**: if you get paid in the same currency you spend, conversion cost is zero — no service beats not converting.

## A two-minute routine before any large transfer

1. Look up the **mid-market rate** for your pair (that is what Fxverter shows).
2. Ask each provider: **how much exactly will arrive**, in the recipient's currency, all-in?
3. Compute the gap between step 1 and step 2 as a percentage — that is the true cost.
4. On recurring transfers, redo the check occasionally; pricing changes.

The mid-market rate is the anchor for all of this: it is the price banks charge each other, the best rate any retail customer can realistically approach, and the reference that makes every quote comparable. Whoever quotes closest to it — after all fees — is giving you the better deal.
